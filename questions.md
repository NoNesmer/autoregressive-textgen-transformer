# Q&A Preparation — Character-Level Transformer for Shakespeare Text Generation

This is a defense-prep guide. Questions are grouped by category; each answer is short enough to say out loud, with a follow-up "explanation" if pressed.

---

## A. Research question, motivation, scope

**A1. What is your research question?**
How do different sampling strategies (temperature and top-k) affect the trade-off between *quality* and *diversity* in text generated by a small character-level Transformer trained on a single-author corpus — and does the shape of that trade-off survive a capacity increase (baseline vs. larger model)?

**A2. Why is sampling worth studying separately from model architecture?**
Even with a fixed model, decoding choices change output dramatically: greedy/very-low T loops; very high T becomes incoherent. Sampling is a cheap, post-hoc knob, so understanding its effect is more practically useful than chasing PPL. Holtzman et al. (2020) show that argmax decoding from well-calibrated LMs degenerates — the choice of sampler matters as much as the LM.

**A3. Why character level rather than word/BPE?**
Three reasons: (1) tiny vocabulary (~40 chars after normalization) keeps the model CPU-friendly and reproducible, (2) it removes tokenizer confounds so the focus stays on decoding, (3) character-level lets the model invent words and renders sampling pathologies (loops, gibberish) very visible. Trade-off: much harder long-range structure than BPE.

**A4. What did you intentionally NOT try to do?**
SOTA performance, nucleus / top-p sampling, beam search, or a multi-author corpus. Scope was deliberately narrowed to a clean baseline + a controlled (T, k) sweep + one capacity ablation.

## B. Data & preprocessing

**B1. Where does the data come from and what license?**
Project Gutenberg eBook #100 — *The Complete Works of William Shakespeare*. Public-domain text used under Project Gutenberg's terms (educational, non-commercial). The Gutenberg header/footer is stripped; acknowledgement is in the README and final report.

**B2. What's the preprocessing pipeline?**
`src/prepare_data.py`: download → strip Gutenberg `*** START OF` / `*** END OF` boilerplate → normalize (BOM removal, lowercase, CRLF→LF, tabs→spaces, smart quotes/dashes folded to ASCII) → build char vocabulary from `sorted(set(text))` → encode to int64 → 80/10/10 contiguous split → save `train/val/test.bin` plus `vocab.json`.

**B3. Why a contiguous (chronological) split, not random shuffle?**
For a language model, random shuffling of overlapping contexts would leak n-grams across train/val/test, inflating metrics. A contiguous slice keeps the splits statistically independent at the document level.

**B4. How big is the vocabulary?**
Built dynamically — on the order of low tens of unique characters (letters, digits, punctuation, whitespace, newline). Exact size is read from `data/processed/vocab.json` at runtime, never hard-coded in YAML.

**B5. What is the block size and why 256?**
`block_size = 256` characters — short enough for CPU-friendly training, long enough that several lines / a stanza fit in context. Used as both the training context and the generation context window.

**B6. What does `stride: 8` mean in `configs/baseline.yaml`?**
Training samples are extracted with a stride of 8 chars, not the full block size — i.e., heavily overlapping windows. This multiplies the effective number of training examples (by ~32×) and is a form of data augmentation. With `stride=block_size` the windows would be non-overlapping (default fallback in `CharDataset`).

## C. Model architecture

**C1. Describe the model in one breath.**
Decoder-only causal Transformer (GPT-style): char embedding + learned absolute positional embedding → N pre-norm Transformer blocks (multi-head self-attention with a triangular causal mask + 4× GELU MLP, residual connections, dropout 0.1) → final LayerNorm → linear head whose weights are tied to the input embedding. Output is logits over the character vocabulary.

**C2. Baseline vs. large configuration?**
- Baseline: 2 layers, d_model=128, 4 heads, block 256.
- Large: 4 layers, d_model=256, 4 heads, block 256.
Same dropout (0.1), same optimizer settings, same seed (42).

**C3. Why pre-norm rather than post-norm?**
Pre-norm (LayerNorm before attention/FFN inside the residual) gives more stable gradient flow at small width and lets you train without warmup tricks. Post-norm — the original Vaswani et al. layout — is sensitive to learning rate at small scale.

**C4. Why weight tying between input embedding and output head?**
Press & Wolf (2016): sharing parameters between the embedding and the output projection regularizes the model, reduces parameter count, and typically improves perplexity. Implemented as `self.head.weight = self.token_embedding.weight` in `src/model.py`.

**C5. Learned vs. sinusoidal positional encoding?**
I use *learned absolute* positional embeddings of size `block_size × d_model` (GPT-style). Simpler and well-suited at this scale. Limitation: it cannot extrapolate beyond `block_size`; at inference longer than 256 chars the context is *trimmed* to the last 256.

**C6. Why GELU, why FFN-4×?**
GELU is the de facto activation in modern Transformers (smoother than ReLU, well-behaved gradients at zero). The 4× hidden expansion in the FFN is the canonical Transformer ratio (Vaswani et al.).

**C7. How is the causal mask implemented?**
A lower-triangular `block_size × block_size` mask is registered as a buffer, sliced to the current sequence length, and used to set upper-triangular attention scores to `-inf` before softmax — so position *t* never attends to positions > *t*.

**C8. How many parameters?**
Logged at the start of training (`Model parameters: …`). Roughly: per Transformer block parameters scale as ~12·d² (4d² for QKV+out projections + 8d² for the FFN). Baseline ≈ a few hundred thousand; large ≈ a few million — about ~8× the baseline.

**C9. What's the autoregressive objective?**
Maximize log-likelihood of the corpus under the chain rule: log p(x₁…xₙ) = Σₜ log p(xₜ | x<ₜ). Equivalently, minimize average per-token cross-entropy under teacher forcing.

**C10. What is teacher forcing — and what's its weakness?**
At training time the model receives the *ground-truth* prefix as input and predicts the next char in parallel for all positions. Its weakness is **exposure bias**: at inference the model conditions on its own (possibly wrong) past outputs, a distribution it never saw during training. Stochastic decoding partly hides this; greedy decoding can exacerbate it.

## D. Training

**D1. Loss, optimizer, schedule.**
Cross-entropy loss with mean reduction during training; AdamW (lr 3e-4, weight decay 0.01); cosine LR schedule across `epochs`; gradient clipping at L2 norm 1.0 each step; early stopping on validation loss with `patience=4`.

**D2. Batch size and length?**
Batch 128, block 256 → 32,768 tokens per batch.

**D3. How is reproducibility enforced in training?**
`seed: 42` from YAML drives Python `random`, NumPy, PyTorch CPU+CUDA seeds, the DataLoader's `torch.Generator`, and `cudnn.deterministic = True` / `benchmark = False`. Same checkpoint produces bit-equivalent evaluation JSON given the same library stack.

**D4. Did you do any sanity check before full training?**
Yes: `--overfit_tiny` runs 500 steps on a single batch and confirms the loss collapses, proving the architecture and gradient flow are correct.

**D5. What did the training curves look like?**
Both runs show monotone decreasing train CE and val CE with a small generalization gap (CSV logs: train 1.41 / val 1.50 for baseline at the last epoch; train 1.05 / val 1.35 for large). The large run's early stopping fires earlier (epoch 8 vs 12), consistent with faster fitting at higher capacity.

**D6. Did the large model overfit?**
Train–val gap is wider for `large` (1.05 vs 1.35) than for baseline (1.41 vs 1.50). Not catastrophic overfitting — early stopping caught it — but yes, the large model's gap is bigger. That is one explanation for the observed sampling behavior at T=1.0 (see F2).

**D7. Why early stopping rather than training longer?**
Validation cross-entropy was the basis for keeping the best checkpoint; once it stopped improving for `patience=4` epochs, more compute was unlikely to help on this corpus. Early stopping is also a regularizer.

## E. Decoding strategies

**E1. Define greedy / temperature / top-k formally.**
Let `z` be the logit vector at step *t*.
- Greedy: `argmax(z)`.
- Temperature: sample from `softmax(z / T)`. T<1 sharpens, T=1 is the model's native distribution, T>1 flattens toward uniform.
- Top-k: keep the k largest logits, set the rest to −∞, then temperature-sample from that restricted softmax.

**E2. Why does greedy degenerate?**
A locally most-likely next token is not globally most-likely; argmax pushes the trajectory into a local mode and tends to fall into high-probability loops (the same n-gram repeats). This is the "Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" finding (Holtzman et al. 2020).

**E3. Why use temperature *and* top-k together (in the heatmap)?**
Top-k alone constrains the *support* of the distribution but still uses raw logits' relative ratios. Temperature alone scales those ratios but never zeroes out junk in the long tail. Combining them — top-k filters the tail, T controls sharpness inside the kept set — is what `topk_sample` in `src/sampler.py` does and what the heatmap explores.

**E4. Why no nucleus (top-p)?**
Out of the proposal's scope. It would be the natural next experiment — nucleus sampling adapts the support per-step based on cumulative probability rather than a fixed `k`.

**E5. Numerical gotchas in your sampler?**
`temperature_sample` falls back to greedy if `T ≤ 0`. `topk_sample` uses `max(T, 1e-8)` to avoid division by zero, then `scatter_` to place the top-k logits in a `-inf` mask before `softmax`. PyTorch's softmax is internally log-sum-exp stable.

**E6. How is generation longer than `block_size` handled?**
Sliding window: each step the model conditions on the **last** `block_size` characters of the running output (`context[:, -block_size:]` in `src/generation.py`). Earlier characters are silently dropped — long-range coherence beyond 256 chars is unsupported by design.

## F. Evaluation metrics

**F1. Define perplexity.**
Perplexity = exp(average per-token cross-entropy on held-out data). Intuitively the geometric mean of "1 / probability assigned to the true next char". Lower is better. **PPL does not depend on the decoding strategy** — it's a property of the model and the data.

**F2. Why is character-level PPL so low (≈4)?**
Because the vocabulary is ~40 chars; uniform-baseline PPL is just |V|. A PPL of 3.98 on a ~40-char vocab is *not* directly comparable to a 4 PPL on a BPE LM — it's a different denominator entirely.

**F3. Define distinct-n.**
`distinct_n(text) = |unique character n-grams| / |total n-grams|` over the generated continuation. Higher = more surface-level lexical/character variety. Reported for n=2, 3.

**F4. Define 4-gram repeat fraction.**
Fraction of sliding 4-gram positions whose 4-gram already appeared earlier in the same generated string. Lower = less immediate self-repetition. `first_repeat_position` (-1 if none) tells *where* repetition first sets in.

**F5. Are these "quality" metrics?**
No — they're proxies. Distinct-n and repeat fraction approximate *diversity* and *anti-loop* behavior, not literary quality. The report is explicit about that and pairs them with reading samples and a small human-eval pilot.

**F6. What about BLEU / ROUGE / human gold standard?**
Inappropriate here: there's no reference continuation for an open-ended Shakespeare prompt. Reference-based metrics would be misleading; that's why I use intrinsic distributional metrics + perplexity + a small Likert pilot.

**F7. Why Spearman rank correlation across model sizes?**
Spearman ρ tests whether the *ordering* of settings by a metric is preserved across capacities, independent of absolute level shifts. This is exactly the question I want to answer: "does the trade-off shape survive scale?"

## G. Results — interpretation

**G1. Headline result?**
Decoding structure is largely preserved across capacities. Spearman ρ across temperature sweeps is ≈1.00 (distinct-3 and repeat-fraction); top-k is ≈0.80 for distinct-3 and ≈1.00 for repeat-fraction; flattened (T,k) heatmap cells ≈0.92. So: which settings are "more diverse" or "more repetitive" is stable, even as raw scores shift.

**G2. The large model has *lower* distinct-3 and *higher* repetition at T=1.0 — isn't bigger supposed to be better?**
That's the most counter-intuitive finding and the most likely Q&A target. Explanation: a better-fitting LM has a *sharper* per-step distribution (lower entropy on training-style continuations). At a fixed T=1.0 that sharpness translates into more deterministic, more repetitive samples — exactly what we already saw at very low T on the baseline. Lower CE ≠ "better samples" at the same sampling knob; if you want matched diversity you need to *raise T* or *raise k* on the larger model. This is consistent with the broader literature: capacity and decoding interact.

**G3. What does the temperature curve look like qualitatively?**
Distinct-2/3 increase monotonically with T; 4-gram repeat fraction decreases. Around T≈1.3–1.5 diversity is high but local coherence visibly degrades in samples. T≈0.7–1.0 is the readable region.

**G4. What does the top-k curve look like?**
At k=1 (i.e., greedy) we see severe repetition. From k=5 to k=20 diversity rises sharply and repetition drops. From k=20 to k=50 returns diminish — additional support is mostly low-probability tail.

**G5. Where on the (T,k) grid would you actually generate from?**
Around T=1.0 with k=20, or T=0.7 with k=50 — both are in the "readable but not deterministic" pocket of the heatmap.

**G6. Greedy test perplexity is ≈3.98 for large vs ≈4.38 for baseline — but you said PPL is decoder-independent. What gives?**
The "greedy" tag in the JSON name refers to the eval *script* also producing greedy continuations. The PPL number itself is computed with teacher forcing on the test set in `compute_perplexity` (`src/metrics.py`) — independent of the decoding strategy.

**G7. Is the large model's better PPL but worse diversity a contradiction?**
No: PPL says the large model assigns higher probability to ground-truth Shakespeare characters. That's strictly about *fit*. Diversity at fixed T says how wide the sampler's effective support is. A sharper, better-fitting model produces a narrower sampling distribution at the same T — those are consistent observations, not opposites.

## H. Ablation & validity

**H1. What is the ablation in this project?**
Hold corpus, pipeline, optimizer, seed, prompts, sampling grid constant. Vary only **model capacity** (baseline 2×128 → large 4×256). This isolates the effect of capacity on the *shape* of the decoding curves.

**H2. Threats to validity?**
- Single corpus, single author — results may not generalize.
- Single seed — variance estimates only come from 3 stochastic samples per cell, not full re-training.
- Distinct-n and repeat-fraction are surface metrics — high distinct-n on gibberish still scores well.
- Human eval is n=15, single rater, not blind — pilot only.
- Large model trained 8 epochs vs 12 (early stopping); not a full epoch-matched comparison.

**H3. Is the comparison really fair if the large run stopped earlier?**
Both runs use their own `best_model.pt` (lowest val loss), which is the standard protocol. If anything the large run benefits from less overtraining. The Spearman analysis is robust to this because it depends on rank ordering, not absolute fit.

**H4. Why 10 prompts and 3 samples per setting?**
Compromise between coverage (more prompts ⇒ more stable per-cell mean) and compute. With 10 × 3 = 30 generations per cell of 512 chars, distinct-n and repeat-fraction means stabilize enough to read trends.

## I. Human evaluation

**I1. What does the human-eval pilot do?**
`scripts/prepare_human_eval.py` produces 15 fixed continuations from the baseline (deterministic seeds) and a CSV with three Likert columns (1–5): *coherence*, *subjective diversity*, *overall*. After rating, `scripts/correlate_human_eval.py` computes correlations between human ratings and `distinct_3` / `repeat_fraction`.

**I2. Limitations of the human eval?**
n=15, single rater (the author — bias disclosure printed on the sheet), not blinded, not pre-registered. Cited as a *pilot*, not as evidence. Correctly framed as such in the report.

**I3. How would you scale it?**
Multiple raters, blinded (no setting label visible), inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff's α or similar), more samples, balanced across (T, k) cells.

## L. Conceptual / theory questions the instructor might lob

**L1. What's a Transformer in one sentence?**
A stack of residual blocks where each block does a multi-head self-attention (token-to-token mixing weighted by learned similarity) and a position-wise MLP, both with LayerNorm.

**L2. What does self-attention compute mathematically?**
For each token: Q·Kᵀ / √d_k → softmax → weighted sum of V. The √d_k scaling keeps the dot product variance roughly constant as d_k grows, preventing softmax saturation.

**L3. Why multiple heads?**
Each head learns a different attention pattern (e.g., one head tracks recent context, another tracks repeats). Concatenating heads gives the model multiple "subspaces" of relations at the same layer cost.

**L4. Why is autoregressive (causal) attention enough — why not bidirectional?**
The training objective is next-token prediction, which by definition cannot see future tokens. Bidirectional attention would leak the answer.

**L5. How does the decoder-only Transformer differ from the original Vaswani encoder-decoder?**
No encoder, no cross-attention. A single causal stack predicts the next token from past tokens — the GPT family.

**L6. What is the role of residual connections + LayerNorm?**
Residuals make optimization tractable in deep networks (gradient highways); LayerNorm stabilizes per-token activations across the embedding dim and makes the loss landscape better-conditioned at small width.

**L7. Why does dropout help here even though the model is small?**
The model still has enough capacity to memorize, especially with stride-8 overlapping windows. Dropout (0.1) regularizes; without it you'd see the train–val gap widen and samples would look like memorized n-grams.

**L8. What happens at T → 0 and T → ∞?**
T → 0: distribution collapses to argmax (greedy). T → ∞: distribution flattens to uniform over the vocabulary; output becomes random characters.

**L9. Cross-entropy vs. perplexity?**
Cross-entropy in nats per token is the loss; perplexity is `exp(CE)`. PPL is a more intuitive "branching factor" — a PPL of 4 says the model is on average as uncertain as a uniform choice over 4 symbols.

**L10. What's the difference between distinct-n and entropy?**
Distinct-n counts *types-vs-tokens* on the surface n-grams of *one continuation*. Entropy of the per-step distribution measures the model's *predictive* uncertainty before sampling. Different things; distinct-n is downstream.
